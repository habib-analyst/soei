                                                                 Abstract
AI coding agents now perform long, multi-step and multi-session software engineering work with limited human
supervision. When a developer returns to such work, the decisive question is not “what did the model emit?” but “what
was actually done, by whom, and can I trust that it is finished?”. We study this question in two ways. First, we build a
working prototype of our proposed system and run it on a real corpus of 45 coding-agent sessions (102,233 raw
records; 20,577 tool executions; 2,336 human-to-agent episodes; April–October 2026). With a blindly audited classifier
(precision 83.3% / 96.7%), we estimate that 23.4% [95% CI 17.1–35.7] of code-editing episodes end without any
independent verification after the last edit, and that 57.8% of to-dos the agent marked complete after editing had
no proof between edit and completion. A 10-minute timing heuristic for session hand-offs recovers only 6 of 24 (25%)
session pairs that our content-lineage analysis links, because 95.5% of real cross-session artifact reuse happens more
than ten minutes apart. 42% of sessions span more than 24 hours, and 48.1% of transcript bytes lie beyond a 2 MiB
replay bound. Credential-like strings appear in 1,326 raw records across 16 of 45 sessions. Second, we propose Soei
(Structured Observability of Evidence and Intent): an acknowledgement-aware event schema, a hash-chained local
evidence ledger, a W3C-PROV-aligned evidence graph, calibrated inference for attribution, drift and lineage-based
hand-offs, and a verification layer that separates an agent’s claim of completion from proof. On the real corpus, the
prototype’s ledger is 20% of the raw transcript size, stores zero content and zero credential matches, verifies at about
126k events/s, detects tampering, and replays deterministically. We formalise the model, specify the architecture in
implementation detail, and define a reproducible evaluation agenda. Finally, we argue that this evidence substrate is a
prerequisite for a golden harness for evaluating and certifying coding agents, and for the governance of autonomous
agents. We define autonomy levels with evidence gates and harness metrics whose baselines come from our data.

Keywords: AI coding agents; agent observability; provenance; verification; software engineering; situation awareness;
empirical study; human–AI collaboration.


  Key empirical findings (real data, this paper)
   1. The claim–proof gap is real: about one in four code-editing episodes ends unverified (23.4%, CI 17.1–35.7%).
      Across 4 classifier versions the raw rate stays between 25.1% and 42.1%.
   2. “Done” is not proven: 186 of 322 (57.8%) to-dos completed after edits had no verification between the edit and
      the “completed” status.
   3. Hand-offs are slow, not fast: real cross-session reuse has a median gap of 8.8 days. A ≤10 min temporal heuristic
      finds 25% of linked pairs; content lineage finds all 24.
   4. Bounded history loses most of the evidence: 19/45 sessions exceed 24 h, and 48.1% of bytes fall beyond a 2 MiB
      replay window.
   5. Acknowledgement matters: 40 of 3,548 file mutations (1.1%, in 30 episodes) were requested but never
      succeeded. A start-event observer would show them as applied.
   6. Raw agent logs are a liability: 1,326 records in 16/45 sessions contain credential-like strings (208 high-
      confidence token matches, 5 distinct tokens). The Soei ledger contains none.
   7. Provenance is cheap: the hash-chained ledger costs 20% of raw size and verifies at about 126k events/s in pure
      Python, with deterministic replay and tamper detection.

1 Introduction
Coding agents have progressed from single-turn completion to long-horizon work. They read repositories, plan, edit
many files, run tools and tests, and increasingly operate as several sessions or sub-agents. Issue-resolution benchmarks
such as SWE-bench [1], agent–computer interfaces such as SWE-agent [2] and reason-act tool use [3] have driven rapid
capability gains. The supervisory side of human–agent collaboration has received far less engineering and scientific
attention. A developer returning to a running agent must rebuild state from scrolling transcripts across terminals. This
maps directly onto the three levels of situation awareness (perception, comprehension, projection) [4].
Two families of tools address the problem in part. LLM trace dashboards (LangSmith, Langfuse, Arize Phoenix) and
the OpenTelemetry generative-AI semantic conventions [5] record model calls, prompts, latency and token cost. They
answer “what did the model do?” but not “which part of my project moved, and is it really done?”. Project-level
observability tools watch the repository, a plan file and whatever agent events a vendor exposes, and visualise progress
at the level of components and tasks. The better ones run locally and stay read-only. None of them, as far as we can
find, measures how far what they show is from what actually happened.
We argue that visibility alone does not give a supervisor what they need. The evidence behind each view has to be
trustworthy, and that is a testable claim. We therefore (i) measure the evidence problems that observability must solve
on a real corpus of coding-agent sessions, and (ii) design, prototype and evaluate Soei, an architecture that treats agent
reports as claims, stores evidence before inference, and derives every view from one tamper-evident record. Several
2026 papers reach related conclusions from other directions (§2.6); ours is, to our knowledge, the first to measure the
claim–proof gap and the hand-off recall problem on real working sessions and to build the substrate as a passive
observer of unmodified vendor agents.

2 Background and related work

2.1 Coding agents and their artifacts
Modern coding agents interleave reasoning and tool actions [3] through repository-specific interfaces [2] and are
measured on issue resolution [1]. In practice they run inside vendor tools (VS Code Copilot agent, Codex CLI/desktop,
Claude Code, Cursor) that persist session logs and expose hooks and native to-do lists. These artifacts are the raw
material for observability. They are also vendor-specific and incomplete, and their format changes without notice. As
we show in §10.6, they may also contain secrets.

2.2 Tracing, clocks and observability
Distributed tracing (Dapper [6], later OpenTelemetry) models work as spans with causal parents and propagated
context. LLM observability adapts this to model and tool calls, and the OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions [5]
standardise model-invocation attributes. AgentTrace [23] instruments agents at runtime with structured security-
oriented logs, and OpenRath [21] proposes a first-class Session runtime value that is branchable, inspectable and
replayable, so that transcripts, tool effects and lineage stop being recorded in separate places. Both require the agent to
be built on, or instrumented by, their framework. These approaches assume instrumented code paths and centre on
requests rather than on project intent (plans, components, deliverables). Logical clocks [7] and hybrid logical clocks
[11] address ordering across sources without synchronised time. That problem returns when provider logs, file-system
events and CI results are fused.

2.3 Provenance
The W3C PROV model [8] represents entities, activities and agents with relations such as wasGeneratedBy, used,
wasAttributedTo and wasDerivedFrom. Provenance is a natural basis for questions such as “which session produced
this exact file version, and did another session consume it?”. We show empirically that these questions cannot be
answered from timing alone.

2.4 Visualisation and situation awareness
The “overview first, zoom and filter, then details on demand” principle [9] and Endsley’s situation-awareness model [4]
set principled targets for a supervisory display: perception of current activity, comprehension of progress relative to
intent, and projection of what happens next. SeaView [30] visualises SWE-agent trajectories for post-hoc error analysis;
it reads benchmark trajectories rather than live developer sessions and does not separate claims from evidence.

2.6 Concurrent work (2026)
Several papers published while this work was in progress treat agent output as a claim that needs independent evidence,
and we position Soei against them here. Proof-or-Stop [18] gates lifecycle transitions (reviewed, tested, done, ready-to-
merge) on fresh, source-state-bound, mechanically checkable evidence, and the runtime-contract position paper [19]
argues for an “evidential face” of agent safety that gates task submission on test runs, log captures and diffs. Both
enforce. Soei’s core is read-only by design (P1) and its contribution is to measure how large the gap is in ordinary use
and to build the evidence substrate that such gates would consume; the policy plane we propose in §13.2 and §14 is
where enforcement would live. Artifact-centred claim-aware observability [20] makes the same
claim/artifact/relation argument for autonomous scientific agents; we make it for software work, add acknowledgement
and hand-off lineage, and quantify it. Skill Runtime Intelligence [24] passively reconstructs Skill-lifecycle stages
across heterogeneous coding-agent harnesses with four evidence grades and keeps unsupported stages as unknown,
which is the closest match to our evidence lattice and adapter stance; it targets Skill activation rather than project-level
claims, hand-offs or history integrity. Auditable Agents [22] defines auditability dimensions (action recoverability and
others) that our four governance questions in §13.1 map onto; the hash-chained ledger and deterministic replay are one
concrete way to satisfy them. On the empirical side, AgentLens [25] finds that 10.7% of passing OpenHands
trajectories on SWE-bench Verified show regression cycles, blind retries or missing verification, which corroborates
Finding 1 from a benchmark rather than a workplace corpus, and a large study of agent Skills [26] finds credential
leakage through privileged skill code, a different channel from the transcript leakage we measure in §10.6. TraceTrial-
Bench [27] and HarnessAudit [28] benchmark harness reliability and mid-trajectory safety, which is the kind of
evaluation a golden harness (§13.2) would run; SWE-Critix [31] trains trajectory verifiers that could replace our rule-
based proof classifier (§14, item 3). A project-level governance manifest for agent-mediated OSS contributions [29]
and a systematisation of execution-security work for coding agents [32] frame the governance side. Table 2 summarises
the differences.

4 Design principles
1. P1 Observe, never act. Soei never edits or approves agent state, and it never prompts the agent.
2. P2 One record, many views. Every view is a deterministic projection of one ledger.
3. P3 Evidence before inference. Provenance-tagged evidence is persisted before interpretation, so inference can be
   re-run later with better rules and re-scored against the same evidence.
4. P4 Claims are not proofs. Agent-reported state is a claim. Completion is upgraded only by independent evidence.
5. P5 Calibrated uncertainty. Inferred relations carry measured confidence, and the system abstains when that
   confidence is below threshold.
6. P6 Private by construction. One field policy is enforced at ingest, before persistence. Secrets never enter the
   ledger.
7. P7 Vendor-neutral adapters. Provider formats are untrusted, versioned inputs validated against a conformance
   corpus.
8. P8 Evaluation-first. Recording, annotation, replay and scoring are first-class features.

5.2 L1 Ingest: adapters, Soei Event Schema and acknowledgement
Adapter contract. An adapter implements detect(sample) → (format, version, confidence), tail(cursor) →
(records, cursor') and normalize(record) → SES[]. Cursors are (path, inode/file-id, size, byte offset). A rotated
or truncated source is detected from the identity and size and re-anchored, never re-read blindly. Each adapter ships
with a conformance corpus of redacted real records per supported format version. CI runs every adapter against every
corpus on Windows, macOS and Linux. Unknown formats produce evidence_class=unknown events and are never
guessed. Our prototype implements the VS Code Copilot agent adapter (session, prompt, message, tool start/complete
with call ids and success flags).

SES envelope. CloudEvents-style [10] fields (id, source, type, time) plus source_offset, hlc, evidence_class, ack,
subject (session, task, artifact digest, activity) and policy (App. A).

Acknowledgement tracker. Every state-changing request (edit, plan update, to-do change) is recorded as proposed at
start and resolved by its matching completion: acknowledged (success), rejected (failure) or timed_out (no completion
within a window or before session end). Projections render only acknowledged state by default. In our corpus this
distinction affects 1.1% of mutations (§10.5).
Privacy gate. Runs before persistence. (i) A field allowlist per event type keeps message, prompt, reasoning, command
body and tool output out by default. (ii) Remaining strings are scanned for credential patterns and replaced with typed
placeholders. (iii) Paths are salted-hashed for export, with a local reversible map kept outside the ledger. Because the
gate runs before the ledger, a secret can never be “un-shown” later: it is never stored.

The ledger is an append-only sequence of canonical-JSON SES events grouped into segments (the prototype uses
JSONL; production can use SQLite WAL with the same chain). Each record stores prev and hash = SHA-256(prev ‖
canonical(e)). A segment manifest commits to its head hash, schema and policy versions and source cursors, which
gives lossless resume and integrity for shared datasets. The ledger stores no content: artifacts are referenced by salted
path hash and, in production, content digests (BLAKE3/SHA-256 or VCS blob ids) captured at generation and use.

5.5 L4 Inference engines
   Attribution: maps activities and artifacts to components and roles using plan globs, path features, co-change
   history and operation type. It is a calibrated classifier that abstains below threshold θ. Directory-name role
   heuristics become one feature among several.
   Lineage and hand-off: computes H0–H3 (§6.4) from used/wasGeneratedBy over artifact digests. Our data shows
   content lineage is required, because real reuse is slow (§10.3).
   Drift: undeclared and stale-done drift (§6.5) turn the usual “plan is behind” indicator into measurable signals.
   Attention: ranks read-only alerts: blocked-on-human, stalled task, contested claim (failing proof after a completion
   claim), sustained undeclared work, and returns after long idle periods.

5.6 L5 Verification
The proof classifier labels activities as static (compile, lint, type-check, diagnostics), build (build/package), test (test
runners or test-named scripts), exec (running the changed artifact or its sibling), smoke (import checks), probe (HTTP to
localhost after restart) or UI (browser checks after an edit). The claim–proof matcher then decides Verified, Unverified
or Contested for every claim, and the completion trust τ summarises coverage and recency (§6.6). This is the layer the
real data shows to be missing in practice (§10.1–10.2).

6 Formal model

  Definition 1 (Evidence event and ledger). An evidence event is a tuple e = (id, src, off, κ, λ, σ, α, π): source,
  source offset, kind, hybrid logical timestamp, subject references, acknowledgement state α ∈ {proposed,
  acknowledged, rejected, timed_out, n/a}, and applied policy. The ledger is L = ⟨e1…en⟩ with hi = H(hi−1 ‖ enc(ei)).


  Definition 2 (Evidence lattice). Statements carry a class from Unknown < Inferred < Observed < Reported <
  Verified. For statements about intent, Reported outranks Observed. For statements about the world, Observed
  outranks Reported. Verified is reachable only through a Proof node.

Confidence fusion. For a relation r with independent signals of calibrated confidence cj and reliability weights wj:

  Property 2 (Replay determinism). Given L≤t, inference version v and policy π, the projection is deterministic.
  Empirical check: two independent replays of the real 42,398-event ledger produced identical result digests (§10.7).


  Property 3 (Tamper evidence). Changing any event invalidates every later hash. Empirical check: flipping one
  acknowledgement in the middle of the real ledger was detected (§10.7).


  Property 4 (No silent completion). No task reaches Verified without a Proof node, and no rejected or timed-out

9.4 Classifier audit protocol
The proof classifier is a set of versioned rules. To avoid reporting an artefact of the heuristics, the first author ran blind
audits. For each version a seeded random sample of 30 episodes carrying the label was drawn, and the actions after the
last code edit (tool names and truncated commands only) were inspected to decide whether an independent check really
happened. False negatives drove each revision: v2 added build commands, quoted interpreter paths, import smoke
checks and test-named scripts; v3 excluded deploy/sync invocations; v4 added localhost HTTP probes, browser UI
checks, invocation of edited PowerShell scripts and packaging. Every version is re-run on the whole corpus as a
sensitivity analysis, and the final estimate is corrected for measured precision in both directions.
Two of my own misreadings (first author) shaped these rules more than any design discussion did. In the v1 audit I had
counted python -c import checks and scripts named _tmp_test_* as “no verification” because they did not look like
tests; in this corpus they are one of the main ways an agent checks its own work. The opposite mistake survived to v4:
one scp deploy was read as execution of the changed script, and only reading the raw tool sequence showed that
nothing had run locally. I would not have found either by looking at aggregate rates.

11 Discussion and threats to validity
What the data changes. Three design decisions that were previously argued now have measurements behind them. (1)
Verification is not optional: a quarter of code-changing episodes and over half of agent-completed edit-backed to-dos
lack proof. (2) Lineage must be content-based: timing heuristics see a quarter of real collaboration. (3) History must be
durable and private: long sessions carry most of the evidence and many secrets.

Internal validity. The proof classifier is rule-based. We mitigate this in two ways: blind audits (4 samples of 30) with a
two-sided precision correction and propagated confidence intervals, and a sensitivity analysis across four classifier
versions. The audit also changed our view of what counts as proof. Running the edited script, hitting localhost or
opening the page in a browser is how agents check work in this corpus, and test runners are 4.5% of verification
actions; a classifier built from unit-test conventions alone would have overstated the gap by almost half (42.1% at v1
against 25.1% at v4). Proofs in other channels (e.g., the human testing manually outside the agent) are invisible, which
biases the “unverified” rate upwards from a human perspective but not from an agent-evidence perspective. The latter is
what an observability tool can display. Lineage uses path-level identity, not content digests: overwrites between write
and read could create spurious links, and digest-level lineage is planned. To-do completion uses the agent’s own to-do
tool, whose granularity varies.
External validity. The corpus comes from one developer and one agent product (VS Code Copilot agent mode, which
ran several frontier models), in a post-silicon validation and tooling context. Rates will differ across teams, agents and
domains. We therefore frame the results as an existence and magnitude pilot that motivates design, not as population
estimates. AgentLens [25] reports missing or disordered verification in 10.7% of passing benchmark trajectories with a
different agent and corpus, which suggests the phenomenon is not specific to our setting. The multi-developer study in
§12 addresses generalisation.

Construct validity. “Verified” means an independent check ran after the last edit, not that the code is correct. Soei
never presents τ as a correctness probability.
Ethics and privacy. All analysis ran locally on the authors’ own sessions. No message, prompt, command or file
content was persisted, and only aggregate counts are reported. Credential values were never printed or stored, and
rotation of the affected credentials is required. A multi-developer study requires informed consent and ethics review.

13 Why this is required: the golden harness and governance of autonomous agents

13.1 The autonomy problem
Every episode in our corpus had a human at the end of it, and still about one in four code-changing episodes ended
unverified (§10.1). In that setting the human is the last line of defence, re-reading and re-running what the agent left
behind. As agents become autonomous, running for hours, chaining sub-agents, merging and deploying without per-step
approval, that defence goes away. An unverified hand-back is then a defect escape path. A missed hand-off becomes an
unaccountable change and a phantom edit a corrupted audit trail. A secret in a log becomes an incident. Autonomy
therefore needs a substrate that can answer, for any change and at any later time, four governance questions (compare
the auditability dimensions of [22]):
 1. Accountability: which agent, session and model produced this exact artifact version, and on whose instruction?
 2. Assurance: what independent evidence shows the change works, and is that evidence newer than the change?
 3. Auditability: can the record be shown to be complete and untampered, and can the decision context be replayed
    exactly?
 4. Containment: did any sensitive data enter the record or leave the machine?
Soei’s layers map one-to-one onto these questions: lineage and attribution (L3–L4) for accountability, the claim–proof
verifier (L5) for assurance, the hash-chained ledger and replay (L2) for auditability, and the ingest-time privacy gate
(L1) for containment.

13.2 The golden harness
We use golden harness for a reference evaluation and assurance environment in which (i) every agent action is
captured as verifiable evidence, (ii) every completion claim is checked against independent proof or a task oracle, and
(iii) every run can be replayed and compared bit-for-bit across agent, model or prompt versions. It serves three
purposes: evaluation (how good is this agent on our work?), regression (did the model or agent upgrade make it worse
or less honest?) and certification (is this agent fit for autonomy level Lk on this class of task?). Harness benchmarks
such as TraceTrial-Bench [27] and HarnessAudit [28] supply the task and safety side; evidence-gated lifecycle control
[18, 19] supplies the enforcement side. Soei supplies the record both consume.

15 Conclusion
Local, project-level observability of coding agents is feasible, and developers want it. Our study of 45 real sessions
shows that visibility on its own is not enough. About a quarter of code-changing interactions end unverified. More than
half of agent-completed, edit-backed to-dos have no proof. Timing-based hand-off detection sees a quarter of real
collaboration. Bounded history drops the long sessions that hold half the evidence. Raw agent logs routinely contain
credentials. Soei responds with one principle at each layer: acknowledge before showing, persist evidence before
inferring, scrub before storing, prove before calling something done, and derive every view from one tamper-evident
record. The prototype shows that this costs about 20% of the raw log size and fractions of a second on real data. The
same substrate is what a golden harness and the governance of autonomous agents require: autonomy that is granted on
measured evidence, verified per claim, attributable per artifact, auditable after the fact, and revoked automatically when
the evidence degrades.
